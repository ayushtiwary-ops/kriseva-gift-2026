## 2. Priya Ramanathan, primary persona

Role: Compliance Officer, Meridian Alpha Capital IFSC Private Limited (the FME). Per FACT_CARD B1, both the Compliance Officer and the Principal Officer are mandated by IFSCA and required to be based in the IFSC, and the signature on the quarterly return is personal, not corporate. By regulation she is accountable for the accuracy and timeliness of everything the fund files, and per CANON she is "the maker," the person whose name attaches to every reported decision.

Who she reports to: functionally, the Principal Officer, Rajiv Menon, who holds the second and final signature. Ultimately, she answers to IFSCA directly for what she certifies, not to an internal compliance department. FACT_CARD B4 notes that the real cost of being an FME sits in people and premises, not in the regulatory fee itself; the construction here assumes that at a lean FME running a small number of restricted schemes, the compliance function is a headcount of one, not a team she manages. That assumption is flagged as an open question at the end of this document.

Tools she uses today: spreadsheets, email, shared folders and the administrator's portal, where Northwind's quarterly statements land. No dedicated reconciliation system, no case management layer, no audit trail beyond whatever her mailbox happens to retain.

Her week at quarter end: this is one of four such weeks a year, set by IFSCA's own filing calendar (FACT_CARD M3, M4: returns due 21 April, 21 July, 21 October and 21 January, twenty-one calendar days after each quarter end). In that week she is waiting on the administrator's statement, chasing her own internal numbers, and holding the filing clock in her head, on top of whatever else her role covers day to day.

Her budget authority and its limits: functionally close to none. The FME's direct regulatory cost is small and fixed, USD 3,000 a year plus a new USD 1,000 per scheme per year from FY2026-27 (FACT_CARD B3), while the real money in the entity sits in net worth, KMP salaries and SEZ rent (FACT_CARD B4), none of which she controls. She cannot independently procure a tool. Anything beyond her own labour needs the Principal Officer, or the fund's economics more broadly, to say yes.

What would get her fired: the construction infers this rather than sources it directly, since no FACT_CARD entry states it outright. What is verifiable is the rising cost of error around her: 25 published IFSCA enforcement actions, 19 of them in the first half of 2026 alone, and two of the 25 ending in cancellation of registration (FACT_CARD W1, W2). A materially wrong filing under her personal signature, a missed 21-day deadline, or being personally named in an enforcement action are the plausible failure modes for a role whose defining feature, per FACT_CARD B1, is that the signature is personal.

From FACT_CARD B2: "A change of Compliance Officer is notified to IFSCA within 15 days. The records have to survive 8 years." Put those two numbers next to each other. Her institutional memory has a 15-day expiry against an 8-year obligation. She can leave, or be replaced, on barely two weeks of notice. What she signed has to answer questions for eight years after that. Nothing in her current toolkit, a spreadsheet, an inbox, a shared folder, survives a personnel change. The regulation assumes continuity of record. Her current tools only provide continuity of memory, and only for as long as she is in the chair.

## 3. Rajiv Menon, the economic sponsor

Rajiv Menon is the Principal Officer: per CANON, "the checker, second named signature under maker-checker, legally on the hook for the signed return." Like Priya's, his signature is personal and IFSCA-mandated (FACT_CARD B1). He does not build the reconciliation. He reads Priya's decisions and either signs or does not.

Why his signature, not her workload, is what creates budget: Priya's pain is a workload problem, and FACT_CARD B7, a hypothesis the fact card itself instructs must be labelled as such (built on an assumed salary of 25 lakh rupees a year, about 10,000 rupees a working day), prices that workload at roughly 2 lakh rupees a year even at five reconciliation days a quarter. Nobody signs a new vendor contract to close a 2-lakh-a-year gap. Rajiv's exposure is different in kind, not degree. His name goes on a document he did not build, cannot fully audit in the time available, and is personally exposed on for as long as the record has to survive: eight years (FACT_CARD B2). Enforcement volume is rising fast around him, 25 actions with 19 in six months (FACT_CARD W1), including one documented case of a single entity taking two enforcement actions in four months (FACT_CARD W3). A tool that makes his signature defensible is solving his problem, not hers. He is the one whose approval turns a workflow improvement into a budget line, because he is the one with something to lose that a spreadsheet cannot protect.

## 4. The fund administrator as channel buyer

Northwind Fund Services computes the NAV and produces the quarterly statement. It is not built for Meridian Alpha alone; an administrator of this kind serves many funds across many FMEs by the nature of the business, which multiplies the surface if the administrator becomes the point of adoption rather than each FME buying alone. FACT_CARD B5 supports this as a real, checked gap: of twelve fund administrators and Big Four firms reviewed, zero published a fee schedule for this kind of work. FACT_CARD R5 adds that none of IFSCA's own ten named DRR modules covers lineage, provenance or reconciliation to a golden source. Nobody upstream is pricing, or building, this.

The tension: an administrator that also sold assurance over its own statement would be attesting to the provenance of its own output, marking its own homework. Anita Deshmukh, Northwind's client services manager on this case, sends the statement and issues the revised version, but per CANON she never sees the fund's internal ledger. She has half the picture by construction. An independent layer that pins every number to its source document, the administrator's statement included, and shows plainly where two sources disagree, is credible in a way an administrator-branded assurance product cannot structurally be.

## 5. The anti-persona

Not our buyer: an operations manager evaluating this purely as hours saved.

FACT_CARD B7, a stated hypothesis built on an assumed salary of 25 lakh rupees a year, is explicit about why this fails arithmetically before it fails on any other ground: even at five reconciliation days a quarter, the entire labour content of this problem is about 2 lakh rupees a year, and our own price sits above that (FACT_CARD B8, also a hypothesis: 3 to 12 lakh rupees per entity per year). A buyer optimising for time saved will always find a cheaper alternative: a part-time analyst, a better spreadsheet template, an intern for filing week. That buyer cannot be won on an efficiency pitch, because the efficiency pitch is true, and it is not in our favour.

Naming this is a strength, not a gap. It keeps the sales conversation pointed at the buyer who is actually exposed, Rajiv, on an eight-year signature, rather than the buyer who is merely busy, an ops manager, four weeks a year. It also protects the FACT_CARD B8 pricing hypothesis from being benchmarked against BPO or junior-staff rates, a comparison it would lose.

## 6. Current-state journey: 21 days that end in a signature

Anchor: quarter ended 30 June 2026, Meridian Alpha Opportunities Fund I, return due 21 July 2026 (CANON section 1; FACT_CARD M3, M4). Every figure below is a CANON section 5 canonical number for this fictional case.

**30 June 2026, quarter end.** Northwind's administrator statement and Sentinel Custody's confirmation both cut off at 16:00 IST. At 17:42 IST that same afternoon, a capital call of USD 1,500,000 lands, after both cut-offs. Meridian Alpha's own internal ledger, cut off at 23:59 IST the same day, catches it. Nobody yet knows this one hour and forty-two minutes will matter.

**3 July 2026.** D1, the Northwind administrator statement, version 1, arrives through the administrator's portal. It is formatted, numbered, and carries Northwind's name. It looks authoritative because it is the only document in the stack that looks finished. Priya starts building her return around it.

**First week of July.** The internal ledger export, D3, gets built using version 1 of the administrator statement as the reference point, because version 1 is the only administrator statement that exists yet.

**8 July 2026.** Northwind reissues the statement. D1 version 2 restates the closing NAV, correcting one unlisted holding. Nobody flags that the internal ledger, D3, was built against version 1. Nothing in the system prompts anyone to check; the ledger was already built, the quarter was already moving, and a revised file landing in a portal does not announce what it silently invalidates downstream.

**Mid-July, assembling the return.** Priya lays D1 version 2, D2 the subscription register, D3 the internal ledger and D4 the custodian confirmation side by side. Committed capital: 42.5 million against 45 million. Drawn capital: 17.8 million against 19.3 million. Closing NAV: 21,940,500 against 22,415,000. She cannot tell, from the documents alone, which differences are mistakes and which are two people correctly describing two different moments in time.

**Days later.** She emails Anita Deshmukh at Northwind about the NAV difference. Anita replies, explaining the restatement of the unlisted holding behind version 2. The explanation is real and correct. It exists nowhere except that email thread.

**Close to the deadline.** The complaints-closed field, F4, has no source anywhere: not the administrator statement, not the register, not the ledger, not the custodian confirmation. The filing form has a box for it, and the box will not submit empty. She types a number. Call it a hypothesis, since nobody, including Priya, can verify it: most likely zero, since zero is the path of least resistance under a clock. Nothing in any of the four documents supports that number, or any other number.

**This is the moment of maximum pain.** Not the version confusion, not the disagreeing numbers, not even the signature that follows. It is the cursor sitting over an empty box, a form that refuses to be submitted blank, and a compliance officer inventing a fact under her own name because the alternative is missing a regulatory deadline. That is the pitch's cold open.

**20 to 21 July 2026.** Priya signs her decisions. Rajiv Menon signs second, the maker-checker separation IFSCA already requires of these two roles (FACT_CARD B1). The filed return, and whatever emails, spreadsheets and portal downloads documented the reasoning behind it, get saved into a folder.

**Eleven months later**, the reasoning lives only in Priya's memory, or in an email thread she may no longer be able to find, when someone, an auditor, a successor, a supervisory query, asks why the drawn capital figure was 19.3 million and not 17.8 million.

## 7. The three costs

Not mainly a time cost. FACT_CARD B7, a stated hypothesis built on an assumed salary of 25 lakh rupees a year, already closes that door: even at five reconciliation days a quarter, the entire labour content of this problem prices out to about 2 lakh rupees a year. Selling time saved against a 2-lakh hypothesis, at a price above that number (FACT_CARD B8, also a hypothesis), is a sale nobody who runs the arithmetic will make. The real costs are three, and none of them are hours.

A defensibility cost. Every choice Priya makes between two correct-looking numbers is currently undocumented at the moment it happens. The reasoning exists briefly in her head and in scattered email threads, then decays. When it is asked for later, what should be a two-minute answer becomes an archaeology project, if it is answerable at all.

A continuity cost. FACT_CARD B2's own arithmetic: 15 days to notify a change of Compliance Officer, 8 years the records have to survive. Every quarter that closes without a durable record of why a number was chosen is a quarter the entity is betting on Priya, specifically, still being reachable and still remembering, for up to eight years.

A single-person-concentration cost. There is no second compliance-function person to catch what Priya misses, and no institutional layer between her judgment and the filed return except Rajiv's signature, which checks the decision, not the reasoning behind it. One person's memory is the whole audit trail.

## 8. Future-state journey with ATTEST

Same case, same 21 days, same four fields, mapped to the seven CANON screens, S1 through S7. Read every beat below against one constraint that holds throughout: ATTEST does not remove the conflicts, does not decide anything on Priya's behalf, and does not reduce her responsibility. Every judgment call in this section is still hers to make. What changes is only whether that judgment leaves a permanent, evidenced mark.

**30 June 2026, quarter end.** Same underlying facts: two cut-offs at 16:00 IST, a capital call at 17:42 IST, the ledger cut-off at 23:59 IST catching it. ATTEST ingests all four source documents as they arrive (S1, case dashboard; `POST /ingest`). Nothing about the underlying timing conflict disappears. It is now visible from day one instead of hiding until someone notices.

**3 July 2026.** D1 version 1 is ingested and extracted. Where a single source exists and is unambiguous, ATTEST proposes a value and pins it to the exact source region in the evidence workspace (S2). Behind that proposal, the agent trace screen (S4) shows the model id, the prompt hash and a LIVE or RECORDED badge, so the proposal is inspectable, not asserted. Nothing is final; a model may propose, a model may never decide.

**First week of July.** Because D1, D2 and D3 disagree on committed capital, F1 does not resolve to a single number. It moves to `CONFLICTED` (S1, S3), candidates shown side by side with no default winner. This is the version conflict CANON documents for F1, still unresolved by the system, now visible instead of buried inside a ledger nobody will later remember was built early.

**8 July 2026.** D1 version 2 is ingested as a new, separately versioned artifact, not an overwrite. In the evidence workspace (S2), version 1 and version 2 both stay visible with their own timestamps. F3, closing NAV, is `CONFLICTED` (S3): the two candidates, 21,940,500 and 22,415,000, both shown with their cut-off and version. The correction is not hidden by a portal replacing a file; it is a new artifact sitting next to the old one.

**Mid-July, assembling the return.** Priya opens the case dashboard (S1) and sees, at a glance, four fields in four different states: F1 conflicted (version), F2 conflicted (timing), F3 conflicted (correction), F4 unsupported (missing). A separate risk and anomaly board (S5) runs in the background with its own deterministic indicators, each closed only by a named disposition; it is not one of the four reported fields, but it is one click away if she wants to check the case for anything else worth a second look. For F2, drawn capital, she opens the evidence workspace, sees D1 and D4 both at 17.8 million with a 16:00 IST cut-off and D3 at 19.3 million with a 23:59 IST cut-off, then opens the conflict decision screen (S3): two cards, neither pre-highlighted, no recommended answer. She still has to decide. The system has only made both correct-looking answers, and their timestamps, impossible to miss.

**Days later, instead of an email thread.** Priya still needs Anita's institutional knowledge; the system cannot manufacture what only Northwind knows about its own restatement, so a version of that conversation still happens. What changes is where the outcome lives. Priya records her decision on F3 in the conflict decision screen with a mandatory, non-empty reason: the restatement, the date, the source. The reasoning that used to live only in an email thread now lives in the case record, tied to her name and a timestamp, permanently.

**Close to the deadline, F4.** Priya opens the evidence workspace for complaints closed and finds an empty state: zero of four source documents contain the field, no candidate value, no text box inviting a number. Her only available action is to confirm no source was found and escalate to the administrator, which requires a reason and attaches her name. She still cannot produce a real number, because no real number exists in any document; nobody can. What she can no longer do is quietly type one to satisfy a form. The system refuses on her behalf, on the record, instead of tempting her to refuse quietly and alone.

**20 to 21 July 2026.** Priya's decisions move to `DECIDED`. She cannot sign her own decisions; the sign-off screen (S6) visibly disables that action in her own session, tooltip: maker cannot check their own decision. Rajiv reviews her recorded reasons, not just her conclusions, and signs second, moving the case to `CONFIRMED` and then `SIGNED`. The case seals (S7): every artifact and every state transition chained into one SHA-256 hash. Altering one byte afterward breaks the chain visibly.

**Eleven months later**, the reasoning does not live only in Priya's memory or a thread she may have lost. It lives in the sealed manifest (S7), the pinned evidence (S2) and the recorded reasons (S3) of a case that has not changed since the day it was signed, provably so, because the hash says so. Whoever asks, an auditor, a successor, a regulator, gets an answer that does not depend on Priya still being at her desk, still remembering, or still able to find an old email.

None of the above removes a single decision from Priya's hands. It removes the silence around the decisions she was always going to have to make.

## 10. Jobs to be done

Ranked in the buyer's words. Priya is the operational voice; where the economics diverge, that is noted.

1. **(Drives purchase)** "When someone asks me, months later, to justify a number I signed, I want the reasoning to exist somewhere other than my own memory or a mailbox I might not be able to search anymore, so I am not personally exposed for a decision I can no longer fully reconstruct."
2. "When two sources disagree, I want to see both, with proof of exactly where each came from, so I stop guessing whether it is an error or a legitimate difference."
3. "When a field has no source anywhere, I want the system to refuse alongside me, not hand me a plausible-looking number I will regret."
4. "When I make a decision, I want my reasoning captured at the moment I decide, not reconstructed afterward under deadline pressure."
5. "When I sign, I want to know a second named person actually reviewed my reasoning, not just my conclusion."
6. **(Explicitly not the driver)** "When quarter end comes, I want less manual reconciliation work." Real, true, and per FACT_CARD B7 (a stated hypothesis) worth roughly 2 lakh rupees a year even under generous assumptions. Ranked last on purpose: it is not the job that gets a contract signed.

Who evaluates (hypothesis): Priya, or whoever holds the Compliance Officer function day to day, since she is the one who would use the evidence workspace and the conflict screens every quarter.

Who signs the contract (hypothesis): Rajiv, or whoever holds the Principal Officer role, since section 3 above argues his exposure, not her workload, is what converts interest into budget.

What budget line (hypothesis): unclear. Candidates are a professional or compliance fees line, or a technology or risk line if the entity has one. FACT_CARD B3 and B4 confirm the regulatory fee line itself, USD 3,000 plus USD 1,000 per scheme, is far too small to hold a purchase in the FACT_CARD B8 hypothesis range of 3 to 12 lakh rupees a year; it has to come from somewhere else in the entity's cost structure, and we do not know where.

What blocks it (hypothesis): likely candidates are procurement caution at a small, newly regulated entity, unfamiliarity with an unproven vendor carrying zero customers (FACT_CARD K5), and questions about how fund financial data is handled by a third party.

How long (hypothesis): unknown. No comparable sale exists yet to anchor a cycle length.

Exact questions residency discovery must answer, one per hypothesis above:

1. Is the Compliance Officer actually the day-to-day evaluator, or does a smaller FME route this kind of tool decision through the Principal Officer or an outsourced compliance consultant instead?
2. Does the Principal Officer's personal exposure actually translate into signing authority for a new vendor, or does that decision sit with a board, an investment manager parent entity, or someone else entirely?
3. What specific budget line, if any, currently exists at a GIFT IFSC FME that a purchase like this could draw from, and who controls it?

## Adjudication of the three open decisions (Fable, 2026-08-19)

### Decision 1: the fabrication moment is not the cold open, and it is better used elsewhere

The moment identified in section 6 is the strongest human beat in this entire pack: a compliance officer typing an unverifiable number into a box because the form will not accept an empty one, and signing it under her own name. The recommendation to make it the pitch's cold open is understandable and I am overruling it.

**Two reasons it should not open the pitch.**

1. **Materiality optics.** The field is complaints closed. To a regulator's ear, a complaints count that nobody can verify sounds like a small problem. Drawn capital at USD 19,300,000 sitting in three documents with three different values sounds like a serious one. The cold open has 20 seconds to establish that this matters, and leading with the smallest field on the return works against that.
2. **The cold open's other job is to set up the demo.** The drafted opening names drawn capital, which is the star conflict in the demo 40 seconds later. Opening on a different field costs us that setup and makes the demo's centrepiece arrive unannounced.

**Where it goes instead: into the demo narration, on the abstention shot.** That is strictly better than the cold open, because at that moment the juror is looking at the empty field on screen. The line has maximum force when the thing it describes is visible.

Line to use, spoken over the F4 abstention shot:

> "There is no source for this field. Anywhere. Today, a compliance officer types a number here, because the form will not submit empty, and signs it under her own name. We produce nothing. Zero is the most dangerous answer in regulatory reporting, because it looks like an answer."

**One rehearsal action.** Run both cold opens once on Wednesday, timed, and let the room decide. If the fabrication opening tests better with a live listener, it wins, and this adjudication is wrong. Decide Wednesday night, then freeze.

### Decision 2: the shared names must be flagged out loud

Yes, and this matters more than it looks. Priya Ramanathan is simultaneously the fictional character in the demo data and the name this document uses for the buyer archetype. A juror who hears "Priya, a compliance officer at an FME, faces this every quarter" can easily hear it as a person we met.

**The rule: every spoken reference to Priya is either explicitly the demo character or explicitly a constructed archetype, never ambiguous.**

Safe phrasing on stage: "The persona we built, and she is constructed from public filings and job postings, not from interviews." Then use her name freely.

The strong version of this, and the one that scores on the honesty criterion, is to volunteer the gap before anyone asks: "We have not spoken to a single practising compliance officer. Everything you are about to hear is reconstructed from primary documents. That is the first thing we would fix with the residency."

### Decision 3: do not claim a one-person compliance function

**Overruled, and this one would have been catchable.** IFSCA requires an FME to appoint both a Compliance Officer and a Principal Officer, and both must be based in the IFSC. Saying "a one-person compliance function" to a room containing anyone who knows the FME framework invites an immediate correction, and being corrected on the regulatory basics costs more than the argument gains.

**Use instead: "a small compliance function, often two named people carrying personal accountability."** That is accurate, it preserves the concentration-of-risk argument almost entirely, and it is actually a stronger form of the point, because the maker-checker product mirrors the two-role structure the regulation already requires.

Route the underlying question to residency discovery: how many people actually touch a quarterly return at a typical FME, and does the Compliance Officer have anyone to delegate to. That is a genuine unknown and a good discovery question.
